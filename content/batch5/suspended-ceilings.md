## A ceiling is a system, not a sheet

Suspended ceilings get priced as an area rate more often than any other element on a job. Someone measures the room, multiplies by a number they remember, and books it as a single line. Then the grid arrives short, the perimeter trim is missing, and nobody ordered the hangers.

The reason is that a ceiling is an assembly of five things that are ordered separately: the grid that carries it, the tiles that fill it, the hangers that hold the grid up, the trim that closes the edge, and the integration items for lights, diffusers and access. Each one has its own unit and its own waste characteristic.

This guide works through a lay-in tile ceiling first because it is the most common, then looks at direct fixed plasterboard and MF systems, which are measured differently again.

## What the order actually contains

For a lay-in grid and tile ceiling on a domestic or light commercial job, the material list is:

- Main runners, in 3.6 m lengths, at 1.2 m centres
- Cross tees, in 1.2 m and 0.6 m lengths, forming the module
- Wall angle or shadow trim, in 3 m lengths, around the perimeter
- Hangers: wire, strap or rod, with anchors, at a spacing set by the system
- Tiles, in the module size chosen
- Ancillary: retaining clips, tile bridging, access panels, edge trims
- Integration: light fittings, diffusers, grilles

Most estimators price runners, cross tees, trim and tiles. The hangers and the ancillary clips are the two lines that get left out, and on a 60 m² ceiling the hangers alone are a hundred units.

## Measure the ceiling, then measure it again for the edge

Start with the plan area, length by width, and then make three adjustments.

Deduct openings larger than one module. A rooflight or a large service opening removes grid and tile, and deducting it is legitimate. Deducting a 600 mm square light fitting is not, because the grid still runs around it.

Add the perimeter trim as a linear item, which is the room perimeter plus 10 percent for cuts. On a room with many returns, alcoves or columns, the perimeter is much longer than the plan suggests and the trim is the item that runs out.

Add the edge conditions: where a ceiling meets a wall out of square, or steps up to a bulkhead, there is a shadow gap, a secondary trim, or a plasterboard upstand. Those are linear or small area items that a ceiling rate never includes.

## Module size decides tile count

Tiles are ordered per square metre by module:

| Module | Tiles per m² | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 600 x 600 mm | 2.78 | The standard |
| 600 x 1200 mm | 1.39 | Fewer joints, needs a stronger grid |
| 500 x 500 mm | 4.0 | Small rooms, older systems |
| 300 x 300 mm | 11.1 | Cloakrooms, service areas |
| 1200 x 1200 mm | 0.69 | Large spans, fewer hangers |

For 48 m² of ceiling in 600 x 600 tiles, that is 48 x 2.78 = 133.4 tiles, so 134, plus waste. The waste figure matters more than people expect: mineral fibre tiles are brittle, they mark if handled with dirty hands, and a cut tile at the perimeter is often scrapped rather than used. Allow 7 percent on a square room and 12 percent on a room with lots of edges and services.

## Grid quantities, by the metre

Grid is priced by length, and the pattern sets the quantity. For a standard 600 x 600 module with main runners at 1.2 m centres:

- Main runners: one per 1.2 m of width, running the length of the room. For a 6 m wide room, that is 6 lengths across, each the room length.
- Cross tees at 1.2 m: one row per 1.2 m of length
- Cross tees at 0.6 m: one row per 0.6 m of length, between the 1.2 m tees

A practical shortcut that is accurate enough for estimating: a 600 x 600 lay-in grid uses about 2.0 to 2.3 linear metres of runner and tee per square metre of ceiling. For 48 m² that is 96 to 110 m of grid, which in 3.6 m lengths is 27 to 31 lengths.

That shortcut only holds for a regular rectangle. A room with an L shape, a bulkhead or a diagonal has more trim and more cutting and the figure goes to 2.5 or 2.8 m per square metre.

## Hangers: the line nobody prices

Grid is suspended on hangers from the structure above, at a spacing set by the manufacturer, typically 1.2 m along the main runners and no more than 1.2 m apart.

Working rule: one hanger per 1.2 to 1.5 m² of ceiling area, so 48 m² needs 32 to 40 hangers, each with a wire, a ceiling anchor and a clip. That is a hundred small components, and if the soffit is concrete rather than timber, the anchors are a different product again.

On a ceiling with a deep void, the wire needs to be longer and the hangers need bracing against lateral movement. Deep voids and windy roof spaces are where a ceiling falls down, and the bracing is a real line item.

## A worked example

A 8.0 m by 6.0 m room, 600 x 600 lay-in tiles, 2.7 m ceiling height, with six light fittings, two diffusers and one access panel.

- Area = 48 m²
- Tiles = 48 x 2.78 = 134, plus 8 percent = 145 tiles, so five boxes of 32 or a mixed order
- Grid = 48 x 2.15 = 103 m, so 29 lengths of 3.6 m
- Perimeter trim = 28 m, plus 10 percent = 31 m, so 11 lengths of 3 m
- Hangers = 48 / 1.35 = 36, plus 10 percent = 40 hangers with anchors
- Access panel = 1, with a frame
- Retention clips = one per tile edge, so roughly 140

The light fittings and diffusers are not ceiling material, they are electrical and mechanical, but they cost ceiling labour because every one of them requires the grid to be set out around it. That labour is the reason a ceiling with many services takes longer than its area suggests.

## Lay-in, direct fixed and MF plasterboard

Three systems, three takeoffs, and they are not interchangeable on price.

**Lay-in grid** is demountable, service friendly, and the cheapest per square metre for a large area. It needs 300 mm or more of void and it looks like what it is.

**Direct fixed plasterboard** is board screwed to battens or a frame, skimmed, and painted. It is measured as board area plus a framing or batten allowance, plus plaster and decoration. There is no access once it is closed, so any services above it need to be final before it goes up.

**MF plasterboard ceilings** are a metal frame with plasterboard, giving a flat, jointless surface with a shallower void than a grid. They are measured as frame components (perimeter channel, primary and secondary channels, hangers) plus board plus skim.

The estimating consequence is that a client comparing a lay-in quote with a plastered ceiling quote is comparing a demountable product with a decorated one. If the price looks similar, one of the two is missing a line.

## Services, access and the cost of a busy ceiling

Every service in a ceiling consumes grid and time:

- Light fittings: a grid cut, a support wire, and a cable drop
- Diffusers and grilles: a cut, a frame, and usually a flexible duct above
- Sprinkler heads: a cut and a coordination problem with the grid module
- Access panels: a frame set into the grid, and the tiles around it cut
- Detectors and speakers: small, numerous, and each one a cut

There is a practical rule from site: a ceiling with more than one service per 4 m² starts to dictate the grid layout. At that point the ceiling should be set out by the services, not the other way round, and the estimator should be told which module has been chosen for the lights, because it changes the trim and the hanger count.

## Height, void and the practical limits

A grid ceiling with less than 200 mm of void is difficult to install and impossible to service. Below about 150 mm, a direct fixed system is usually the only option.

Above 4 m, access becomes the dominant cost. A tower or a mobile scaffold is needed, and that belongs in the preliminaries or in the ceiling rate, but it must appear somewhere. Tiles at 5 m are not installed at the same rate as tiles at 2.4 m, and a rate quoted without a height note is a rate quoted for the easy version.

## Productivity and what slows it down

A two person gang installs 30 to 50 m² of lay-in ceiling a day in a clear room with the grid set out and materials at hand. Productivity drops with:

- Services to coordinate, which is the biggest single factor
- Height above 3 m
- Many returns, columns and bulkheads
- Fragile or heavy tiles, such as acoustic or moisture resistant boards
- Working in an occupied building with protection and limited hours

Direct fixed plasterboard is slower per square metre again, because it involves framing, boarding, taping and skimming, and it needs a plasterer after the ceiling gang has gone.

## Waste, storage and breakage

Mineral fibre tiles break. They break in the van, they break when a stacked pallet is leaned, and they break when a plumber stands on the grid to reach a pipe. Allow 7 to 12 percent, more if the site has other trades working above the ceiling after installation.

Store tiles flat, in a dry space, on a pallet. Tiles stored on edge warp, and a warped tile will not sit in a grid without showing a shadow line.

Grid lengths are 3.6 m and awkward to carry through a house. On a refurbishment, check whether they will go up the stairs before the order is placed, because a 3.6 m length will not turn a normal landing.

## Common errors worth checking

1. Pricing a per square metre rate that excludes trim, hangers and clips
2. Using the standard grid allowance on a room with a complicated perimeter
3. Forgetting that light fittings need their own support independent of the grid
4. No access panel where the services above will need attention
5. Missing the height note, so the rate assumes 2.4 m
6. Deducting service openings that still need grid around them
7. Ordering tiles without allowing for breakage on a live site

## Frequently asked questions

**How many tiles per square metre?** 2.78 for 600 x 600, 1.39 for 600 x 1200, and 11.1 for 300 x 300.

**How much grid per square metre?** About 2.0 to 2.3 linear metres for a regular 600 x 600 lay-in ceiling, more on complicated perimeters.

**How many hangers do I need?** One per 1.2 to 1.5 m² of ceiling, with anchors suitable for the soffit material.

**What is the minimum void for a lay-in ceiling?** Around 200 mm to install comfortably and service afterwards. Below 150 mm, use a direct fixed system.

**Can I put lights anywhere in the grid?** In a lay-in grid, lights should sit on the module so the tiles are not weakened. That is why the module is chosen before the lighting layout is fixed.

**How long does a ceiling take?** A two person gang does 30 to 50 m² a day for a lay-in grid, less where services are dense.
